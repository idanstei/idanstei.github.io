**About**

I’m an R&D executive and biomedical engineer with a background in medical imaging, acoustics, signal processing, and the development of diagnostic medical devices.

My career began close to the underlying technology—developing algorithms, imaging systems, and instrumentation—and gradually expanded into clinical translation, product development, and R&D leadership. Along the way, I’ve led multidisciplinary teams working across engineering, physics, software, clinical research, manufacturing, and regulatory disciplines, taking technologies from early research through clinical validation and toward commercialization.

Despite that progression, I’ve always stayed close to the technical problems. I enjoy understanding the physics behind a system, figuring out why something isn’t working, and then building the team and development strategy needed to turn a promising technology into something that can work reliably in the real world.

**Why molecular imaging**

One problem has become particularly important to me: making molecular imaging much more widely accessible.

Many diseases are characterized by biochemical and physiological changes before significant anatomical changes become apparent. Those changes can tell us what is happening in tissue—not simply what the tissue looks like—and can provide valuable information for studying disease, diagnosing it, and monitoring its progression or response to treatment.

Molecular imaging provides access to this kind of information, but many of today's technologies require expensive equipment, specialized facilities, or infrastructure, limiting where and how frequently they can be used.

Ultrasound presents an interesting contrast. It is safe, relatively inexpensive, portable, and already used throughout medicine. Increasingly, ultrasound can move beyond the radiology department to the bedside, primary-care setting, and eventually wherever imaging is needed. But conventional ultrasound primarily provides anatomical and mechanical information.

What if some of the information available through molecular imaging could become as accessible as ultrasound?

That is the problem I want to work on.

**Why thermoacoustic imaging**

I believe thermoacoustic imaging offers a particularly promising path toward that goal.

Thermoacoustics uses electromagnetic excitation and acoustic detection. In many respects, it is closely related to photoacoustic imaging, but using RF rather than optical excitation opens up a different set of possibilities.

Photoacoustics benefits from the tissue's extraordinarily rich optical absorption contrast. Thermoacoustics sacrifices some of that intrinsic contrast but offers important advantages: substantially greater penetration depth, no requirement for high-powered lasers or associated laser-safety infrastructure, and, importantly, a path toward quantitative imaging.

At RF frequencies, electromagnetic wavelengths in tissue are on the order of centimeters, making the excitation field considerably more tractable to model and compensate for than optical fluence deep within heterogeneous tissue. That enables the recovery of quantitative information about tissue properties rather than relying solely on qualitative image contrast.

My work at ENDRA strengthened my conviction that this is not merely a theoretical possibility. We developed and clinically evaluated thermoacoustic technology designed to make quantitative measurements in human tissue, and that experience convinced me that there is much more to explore.

The longer-term potential goes further. Thermoacoustic contrast is not limited to intrinsic tissue properties. Research has already demonstrated thermoacoustic contrast agents, opening a path toward additional molecular specificity.

But I believe the sequence matters.

First, we need the imaging platform.

A practical thermoacoustic system needs to exist, perform reliably, generate quantitative information, and be available to researchers and clinicians. Once that platform exists, there is a reason to develop new contrast agents, applications, and biological tools around it.

That is where I believe the opportunity lies: establishing thermoacoustic imaging as a practical, quantitative molecular-imaging platform and using it to help make molecular imaging far more accessible.

**How I approach R&D**

I’ve spent much of my career at the intersection of physics, engineering, and medicine. That has shaped how I lead R&D.

I believe difficult technology programs require both technical depth and strong execution. The physics has to work. The system has to work. The clinical question has to matter. And ultimately, the technology has to become something that can be manufactured, validated, and used outside the laboratory.

I enjoy working across those boundaries—digging into difficult technical problems when necessary while building and leading multidisciplinary teams capable of carrying the solution much further than any individual engineer or scientist could.

There are many interesting problems in medical technology. But making biologically meaningful imaging information more widely accessible—and continuing to explore what thermoacoustic imaging can contribute to that goal—is one I particularly want to keep working on.
